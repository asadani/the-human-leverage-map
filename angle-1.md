I actually think a **2D map is significantly stronger** than a ladder.

One weakness of most maturity models (including Hawkins, Maslow, Gartner Hype Cycle) is that they imply everyone moves in one direction. Reality is messier.

For example:

* A brilliant AI researcher may have **very high AI capability** but  **poor judgment** .
* A CEO may have **excellent judgment** but almost no AI skills.
* A junior engineer may use AI constantly but still think at a task level.
* A teacher may barely use AI yet create immense human leverage.

A two-dimensional map captures this.

# Why I think this has the potential to become a genuinely original framework

Many existing models answer one question:

* Hawkins: "What is your state of consciousness?"
* Maslow: "What motivates you?"
* Gartner: "Where is the technology?"
* Dreyfus: "How skilled are you?"
* Simon Sinek: "Why do you work?"
* Cal Newport: "How do you produce value?"

None answer the question that defines the AI era:

> **As intelligence becomes abundant, where does uniquely human value come from?**

That's the question your map should revolve around.

If I were co-authoring this with you, I'd make one deliberate design choice: **avoid emotional labels like "Fear" or "Love."** They make the model feel transient and subjective. Instead, use enduring capabilities— **Capability** ,  **Judgment** ,  **Responsibility** ,  **Influence** ,  **Purpose** , and  **Leverage** . Those remain relevant whether AI is GPT-5, GPT-12, or something we can't yet imagine. The map then becomes less about *how people feel about AI* and more about  *how humans evolve when intelligence itself becomes a commodity* . That's a framing I haven't seen articulated in quite this way, and it has the potential to become a durable model rather than a snapshot of today's AI discourse.
